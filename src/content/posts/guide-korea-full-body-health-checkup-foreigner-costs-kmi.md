## The $300 Full-Body Checkup, and Why It's Real (With One Catch)
In the US, a genuinely comprehensive preventive workup — bloodwork, imaging, an endoscopy, a specialist read on all of it — routinely means separate appointments spread across months and a bill that can run into the thousands once you add up an MRI here, a GI consult there. In Korea, dedicated health-screening institutes have turned the same workup into a single, scheduled visit: check in fasting in the morning, move through a sequence of stations, and walk out a few hours later having done in one day what takes most Americans a full season of scheduling.

Here's the catch worth knowing before you book: **the "$300" figure is real, but it's the budget end of a real price range, not a flat number.** Dedicated screening-only institutes — KMI (한국의학연구소) being the largest and best-known — advertise base packages in the roughly $300–400 range (KMI's own basic tier has been reported around $330–400 depending on the exact package and exchange rate) for about 3 hours and 70+ individual test items. Go to a major university hospital's international health center instead — Severance, Samsung Medical Center, Asan, CHAUM — and you're looking at a genuinely different tier, commonly $700 to $2,000+, for a more thorough workup with full-time English-speaking escort service throughout the visit. Both are legitimate; they're just different products. This guide focuses mainly on the budget/mid tier, since that's where the "$300, 3 hours" reputation actually comes from.

## What a Standard Base Package Actually Covers
- **Basic diagnostics:** Body composition analysis, ECG (heart rhythm), chest X-ray, and a vision/hearing check.
- **Lab panels:** A comprehensive blood draw covering liver function, kidney function, thyroid panel, cholesterol/lipid profile, blood glucose (diabetes screening), and a set of tumor marker tests, plus a standard urinalysis.
- **Imaging and ultrasound:** An abdominal ultrasound covering the liver, gallbladder, pancreas, kidneys, and spleen in one pass.
- **Sedation (sleep) gastroscopy (수면 위내시경):** This is the add-on most foreign travelers choose that they'd never bother with at home. Under light IV sedation, the actual scope procedure takes roughly 10 minutes and you're unconscious for essentially none of the discomfort people associate with an unsedated endoscopy — you wake up in a recovery room, not mid-procedure. It's genuinely popular specifically because getting sedated for a routine screening endoscopy is far more accessible (and far cheaper) in Korea than in most Western healthcare systems. One real logistics note: because you're sedated, **you cannot drive yourself afterward** — arrange a taxi, ride-share, or companion for the trip back to your hotel.
- **Colonoscopy:** Usually a separate add-on rather than a base-package default — commonly around an extra $150 on top of the base package — and it comes with its own scheduling reality: proper bowel prep the day before matters as much in Korea as anywhere else, so don't try to add it same-morning as a walk-in. Ask your center whether they can combine it with the gastroscopy under the same sedation session, since many can, which saves you a second recovery window.

## Top Screening Centers and How Foreigners Actually Book
**KMI (한국의학연구소)** is Korea's largest dedicated screening-only network and the one most associated with the budget/efficient end of this market — high volume, standardized packages, "basic" (not fluent, escorted) English support at the base tier. **Hanaro Medical Foundation (하나로의료재단)** sits in a similar price bracket. On the premium end, major university hospitals — **Severance, Samsung Medical Center, Asan Medical Center, Seoul National University Hospital, CHAUM** — run dedicated international/global health centers with full English-speaking coordinators assigned to your visit, at correspondingly higher prices.

### The Real Foreigner Booking Barrier
Most Korean hospital and screening-center booking systems are built around domestic phone-number verification (본인인증) or Korean resident ID, which foreign travelers simply don't have — you can't always just book online the way you would with a US provider's patient portal. Three practical workarounds:
1. Contact the hospital's own **International Health Care Center / Global Coordinator desk** directly by email, which every major hospital in this list maintains specifically for this problem.
2. Go through a medical-tourism concierge platform aimed at foreign patients — Creatrip and Himedi are two English-language examples that handle the Korean-side booking and communication on your behalf.
3. If you already have a Korean contact or hotel concierge, ask them to book by phone.

**Report turnaround:** Budget on roughly **2–3 weeks** for your full English-language report to arrive by email/PDF after your visit — some centers move faster, but don't plan around a guaranteed one-week turnaround, since multi-week is the more commonly reported timeline.

## Scheduling This Into a Travel Itinerary

### Fasting Matters, and It's Not Optional
Standard prep is 8–12 hours of fasting before your appointment — water is generally fine, food and most drinks aren't — because an accurate blood glucose and lipid panel, plus a clean gastroscopy view, both depend on an empty stomach. This is exactly why **morning slots (commonly 7:00–8:30 AM check-in)** are the default and often the only option: you fast overnight and finish by early-to-mid morning rather than fasting through most of your travel day.

### Flight Safety
The non-invasive portions — bloodwork, ultrasound, X-ray, ECG — carry zero flight restriction; you could fly the same day. Sedation is the only piece that needs a buffer: plan on a couple of hours in a recovery area before you're cleared to leave, and give yourself a normal rest-of-day rather than heading straight to the airport, less because of a hard medical rule and more because you'll still feel groggy for a few hours after IV sedation.

## Booking This Safely: Skip the Unlicensed Broker
Exactly as with cosmetic clinic bookings, steer toward your hospital's own official coordination desk or a transparent, named concierge platform — and away from any individual "agent" or broker promising a discounted rate through a personal connection. Soliciting patients for a referral commission is a real legal problem under Korean medical law regardless of the patient's nationality, and an unlicensed middleman gives you no accountability if something about the booking, pricing, or results handling goes wrong. A legitimate concierge platform will say plainly that it's a booking/communication service, not a medical provider itself — that distinction is worth confirming before you hand over a deposit.

On tax refunds: 2026 ended VAT-refund eligibility specifically for **aesthetic** clinic procedures — Botox, filler, cosmetic laser treatments and similar. A comprehensive health checkup is a diagnostic medical service, not an aesthetic treatment, and diagnostic/preventive medical care has generally sat in a different tax category from cosmetic procedures under Korean law all along — it was never part of the refund scheme that ended, rather than being a casualty of it. Tax treatment can still vary by exactly what's bundled into your package, so ask your screening center directly whether VAT applies to your specific invoice rather than assuming either way.
