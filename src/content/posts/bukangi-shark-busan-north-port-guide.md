Busan's most talked-about visitor in autumn 2026 did not arrive on a concert bill or tourism campaign. A shark entered the open canal at **North Port Waterfront Park**, citizens nicknamed her **Bukangi** (부캉이), and the waterfront near Busan Station suddenly became a national news story.

The attention was real, but the situation is easy to misunderstand. Bukangi is a wild animal, not a permanent attraction. Koursea's [live Bukangi visitor guide](https://www.koursea.com/bukangi-shark-busan-north-port-guide.html) carries the latest confirmed status, directions and practical safety information. This article explains the story, why it spread so quickly and how to visit responsibly.

## The verified timeline

Bukangi was first reported in the North Port canal on **September 18, 2026**. Early coverage proposed more than one possible species, so those first identifications should not be repeated as settled fact.

On October 2, Busan City announced a second operation intended to guide the shark safely toward open water. The city said boats, a specially made net and participating agencies would prioritize animal safety. Subsequent attempts did not produce a confirmed return to the sea, and authorities moved to monitoring rather than repeatedly forcing another operation.

The species question became clearer on **October 6**. The National Institute of Fisheries Science examined close footage and identified Bukangi as a **female dusky shark** (*Carcharhinus obscurus*) measuring about three meters.

As of Koursea's October 9 review, we found no later official notice confirming that Bukangi had left the canal. That is not a promise that she will be visible. A wild animal can move, remain below the surface or leave before a public notice is published.

## Why Bukangi became so popular

The setting explains much of the reaction. North Port Waterfront Park is an open public space close to Busan Station, not a distant marine facility. A large wild shark appearing in a landscaped urban canal created an unusual contrast: city skyline, pedestrian park and marine wildlife in one frame.

The affectionate name helped the story travel. “Bukangi” combines **Bukhang**, the Korean name for North Port, with a friendly Korean name ending. Short videos then turned an uncertain wildlife event into something people felt they could visit immediately.

The crowd numbers were substantial. Busan City figures reported by SBS counted **891,800 cumulative visits through October 4**, and later reporting said the total passed one million. Those are park visits, not verified shark sightings. The distinction matters: a viral crowd total does not prove that every visitor saw Bukangi.

## This is not a wildlife show

The most responsible way to understand the phenomenon is as a temporary overlap between an urban waterfront and a wild animal's movements.

- Stay on public paths and behind every barrier.
- Never feed the shark or throw objects into the canal.
- Do not enter the water, climb railings or lean over an unsafe edge.
- Keep staff, police, coast guard and rescue access clear.
- Avoid drones near the animal and any active monitoring operation.
- Do not publish a precise live position during a rescue or crowd-control operation.
- If the front row is too dense to keep children and belongings safe, leave the area.

These rules are not only etiquette. Feeding or crowding can alter animal behavior and complicate monitoring. A dramatic photograph is not worth interfering with a release decision.

## North Port is still worth understanding without a sighting

North Port Waterfront Park is part of Busan's changing harbor landscape and is walkable from Busan Station through the elevated pedestrian connection. It can fit naturally into a station-area route, especially with Choryang or Busan's original downtown districts.

But a tightly timed trip should not depend on seeing one animal. Coastal wind, a large open park and changing crowd controls can make the walk longer than it looks online. If Bukangi is not visible, treat the visit as a waterfront stop rather than waiting indefinitely or moving toward an unsafe crowd.
